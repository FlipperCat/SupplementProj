**What it is:** Fisetin is a plant flavonoid with senolytic properties—meaning it can selectively eliminate senescent (aged, dysfunctional) cells that accumulate with age. Emerging longevity research suggests senolytics may reverse aging processes at the cellular level, offering potential rejuvenation beyond conventional anti-aging approaches.

## What Is Fisetin?

Fisetin is a naturally occurring flavonoid found in strawberries, apples, cucumbers, onions, and other plant foods. While it can be obtained from diet (one strawberry contains ~0.2 mg fisetin), therapeutic doses (100-500 mg) require supplementation.

What makes fisetin unique among flavonoids is its senolytic activity—the ability to selectively target and eliminate senescent cells. Senescent cells are cells that have stopped dividing due to age, damage, or stress. They accumulate over time and secrete inflammatory factors that damage surrounding healthy cells, contributing to aging and age-related diseases.

Fisetin is one of the most potent natural senolytics identified, which is why it's generating significant longevity research interest.

## Benefits

### Senescent Cell Clearance
Fisetin's primary mechanism is clearing accumulating senescent cells:

**Mechanism:** Senescent cells resist normal cell death pathways. Fisetin selectively activates cell death mechanisms specific to senescent cells while leaving healthy cells unharmed. This selective toxicity is why it's called a "senolytic."

**Theoretical benefits:**
- Removes pro-inflammatory cells contributing to aging
- Reduces systemic inflammation from senescent cell burden
- Allows surrounding healthy cells to function optimally
- May reverse age-related tissue dysfunction

**Research stage:** Primarily preclinical (cell and animal studies). Limited human data exists, though early trials show promise.

### Anti-Inflammatory Effects
Beyond senolytics, fisetin has direct anti-inflammatory activity:

- Reduces inflammatory markers (IL-6, TNF-α, NF-κB)
- Supports resolution of chronic inflammation
- May benefit age-related inflammatory conditions
- Synergizes with other anti-inflammatory approaches

### Cognitive & Neuroprotection
Fisetin crosses the blood-brain barrier and supports brain health:

- Neuroprotection from oxidative stress
- Support for cognitive function
- May reduce neurodegenerative disease risk
- Antioxidant support in aging brain

### Cardiovascular Benefits
Emerging evidence for heart health:

- Reduces atherosclerotic plaque formation
- Supports endothelial function
- Anti-inflammatory cardiovascular support
- May improve arterial elasticity

### General Antioxidant Support
Beyond senolytic effects, fisetin is a potent antioxidant:

- Reduces oxidative stress markers
- Protects cellular DNA and mitochondria
- Supports cellular resilience
- Synergizes with exercise and other longevity interventions

## How It Works

### Senolytic Mechanism
Senescent cells have survived beyond normal lifespan by developing resistance to apoptosis (cell death). Fisetin overcomes this resistance through multiple pathways:

1. **HSP90 inhibition:** Senescent cells depend on heat shock protein 90 for survival; fisetin blocks it
2. **PI3K/Akt pathway inhibition:** Reduces pro-survival signaling
3. **Direct apoptosis activation:** Triggers death pathways in senescent cells specifically

This selective toxicity (harmful to senescent cells, harmless to healthy ones) is what makes fisetin effective as a senolytic.

### Inflammation Reduction
Senescent cells are major pro-inflammatory factories, secreting IL-6, TNF-α, and other inflammatory factors. Clearing them reduces this chronic low-grade inflammation ("inflammaging") characteristic of aging.

### Cellular Resilience
Fisetin's antioxidant activity protects healthy cells:

- Reduces reactive oxygen species (ROS)
- Protects mitochondrial function
- Supports cellular stress response
- Enhances survival of healthy cells

## Dosage Recommendations

**Standard longevity dose:** 100-500 mg daily
- 100-200 mg for general anti-aging
- 500 mg for more aggressive senolytic effect
- Can be taken as single dose or split

**Dosing approach:**
- Some recommend intermittent dosing (daily for 2 weeks, then 2 weeks off) to avoid adaptation
- Others recommend daily continuous dosing
- Evidence insufficient to strongly recommend either approach

**With food:** Take with fat-containing meal to enhance absorption (fisetin is fat-soluble)

**Timing:** Any time of day; consistency matters more than specific timing

**Duration:** 8-12 weeks minimum to assess effects (cellular changes require time)

## The Research

**Senolytic Activity:**
- In vitro studies (Mayo Clinic, other labs) confirm fisetin's senolytic activity
- Selective toxicity to senescent cells confirmed across multiple cell types
- Comparable or superior to other natural senolytics (quercetin, dasatinib)

**Human Trials:**
- Limited human data; most evidence preclinical
- Early phase 1b trial (Mayo Clinic, 2022) showed safety and feasibility
- Larger human trials underway
- No serious adverse events reported in trials to date

**Longevity Models:**
- Extends lifespan in animal models (primarily short-lived organisms)
- Reverses age-related tissue dysfunction in animal studies
- Improves physical performance and organ function in aging animal models

**Inflammation Markers:**
- Reduces inflammatory markers in cell culture and animal studies
- Consistent anti-inflammatory activity across research

**Important note:** Most evidence remains preclinical. Human evidence is limited but growing. Fisetin's potential is significant, but it's still an emerging supplement without extensive long-term human data.

## Safety & Side Effects

### Excellent Safety Profile
Fisetin is extremely well-tolerated:

**Side effects (very rare):**
- Mild nausea (if taken without food)
- Rare headache
- No serious adverse events reported

**Management:** Take with food; start with lower dose (100 mg) if sensitive to new supplements.

### Human Trial Safety
Early human trials (Mayo Clinic senolytic trials) showed excellent safety with no serious adverse events even at higher doses (20 mg/kg, ~1,400 mg for 70kg person).

### Theoretical Concerns
**Cellular adaptation:** Very high doses might potentially allow senescent cells to develop resistance to fisetin. However, no clinical evidence of this at recommended doses.

**Intermittent vs. continuous dosing:** Some recommend cycling (2 weeks on, 2 weeks off) to prevent adaptation, though evidence is theoretical.
